# Designing an IDE that withholds the code

A student-leaning AI IDE is a small editor in which the student is the only author and the AI is a read-only office-hours TA: it explains concepts, looks up APIs, explains error messages, reviews code the student has already written, and asks the student to predict and explain, but it cannot edit files, run commands, autocomplete, or emit assignment code. The evidence supports this shape fairly directly. Unrestricted AI help raises performance on the assisted task while leaving unassisted skill flat or lower, including among experienced Python developers learning an unfamiliar library, and the loss concentrates in debugging ([Shen & Tamkin](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)). Stanford CS336 reached the same conclusion on its own and, in Spring 2026, wrote it into a machine-readable `AGENTS.md` that tells any agent to act as a TA that writes no Python or pseudocode ([CS336 CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)). No existing product fills the slot: course tutors are tied to intro courses, vendor "learning modes" are prompt-level toggles that leak and switch off in one click, and mainstream editors offer only AI on or AI off. Two cautions shape the design. Guardrailed tutors have mostly been shown to remove harm, not to beat no-AI study, and heavy Socratic friction drives learners to an unrestricted chatbot, so the friction has to be calibrated and the override deliberate and logged. The build is cheap: a tutor core hosted in an existing editor takes days, model cost for one heavy user is roughly $30-40 a month on a mid-tier Claude model by the research notes' estimate, and GPU rental for the course costs more than the tutor.

## Assisted performance rises while unassisted skill stays flat or falls

The reader's instinct, that tools which write the code are the wrong tools for learning to write it, matches the best causal evidence. In Anthropic's randomized study, 52 developers learned the Python Trio library with or without a GPT-4o chat assistant and then took an unassisted quiz. **The AI group scored 4.15 points lower on a 27-point quiz (Cohen's d = 0.74, p = 0.01), with no significant speed-up**, and the control group scored higher at every experience level. Most participants were experienced: 29 of 52 reported seven or more years of coding. The largest gap was on debugging questions. Control participants hit a median of three errors against one for the AI group, and the authors argue that resolving those errors independently is what built the skill ([Shen & Tamkin](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)). This is the closest available match to a CS336 auditor: a competent programmer, new material, no grade at stake.

Other studies point the same way. In a field experiment with roughly a thousand high-school math students, a ChatGPT-like interface raised practice grades 48% and lowered later unassisted exam scores 17%; a hint-only tutor raised practice grades 127% and left exam scores level with the no-AI control ([Bastani et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486); [Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/)). A preregistered 2026 meta-analysis of programming studies found a pooled learning effect of g = 0.14 with a confidence interval spanning zero, and one decisive moderator: **g = -0.06 when AI was not allowed at the exam versus g = 0.76 when it was** ([Maier et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.04779v1)). Task completion with AI in the loop is therefore not evidence of learning, and a student editor should not treat green tests as proof of understanding.

### What separates the learners who kept their skill

Among AI users, the split is about who does the generative and diagnostic thinking. In the Anthropic data, participants who delegated, who progressively handed more over, or who repeatedly fed errors back to the AI averaged below 40% on the quiz. Those who asked only conceptual questions and wrote the code themselves, or who generated code and then interrogated it, averaged 65% or more. Conceptual inquiry was the most common high-scoring pattern and the second-fastest overall. Retyping AI code instead of pasting it made no notable difference to scores ([Shen & Tamkin](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)). Two independent studies found the same divide: asking for solutions lowered understanding while asking for explanations raised it ([Lehmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09047v2)), and students who used ChatGPT as a tutor retained a gain after a week while those who had it do the work lost a 1.5 SD essay advantage entirely ([Contractor & Reyes](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08849v2)).

These pattern comparisons are descriptive. Participants chose their own pattern, the Anthropic clusters hold two to seven people each, and no study has randomly assigned programmers to "concept-only" versus "generate-then-explain". The design conclusions drawn from them are well-motivated hypotheses, not tested results.

Older learning science predicted all of this. Problem solving before instruction beats instruction first (g = 0.36 across 53 studies), though scaffolded problem solving does as well as unscaffolded struggle, so the supported rule is "attempt first, then get help" and not "struggle alone" ([Sinha & Kapur summary](https://phys.org/news/2021-09-productively-wiser.html); [ETH research collection](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/492056)). Intelligent-tutoring research found hint abuse in 36% of student actions in one geometry tutor ([LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse)), and a 2026 replication with 999 students found that requesting a hint before any attempt was negatively associated with post-test and delayed post-test scores ([An et al., LAK 2026](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs.cmu.edu/Web/People/bmclaren/pubs/AnEtAl-RevisitingTheHintButton-LAK2026.pdf)).

### Where the evidence is thin

Three limits matter for a builder. First, guardrails have reliably removed harm but rarely produced gains. The one large positive result, a Harvard physics tutor at 0.63 SD, depended on expert-written step-by-step solutions and platform-enforced sequencing, measured learning immediately, and covered material its authors do not expect to generalize to complex synthesis ([Kestin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)). Second, horizons are short: the Anthropic study ran about an hour with an immediate quiz, and the longest delayed measures found were one week. Claims about long-term skill atrophy are not established. Third, self-assessment is unreliable. LLM access raised perceived learning beyond actual gains in one experiment ([Lehmann et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09047v2)), and in a study of CS1 students 20 of 21 produced a working program while 10 showed metacognitive difficulties and "finished with an illusion of competence" ([Prather et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.17739v1)). No study covers graduate ML coursework, multi-week projects, or agentic tools; the Anthropic authors expect agents to offload more than chat does but did not test it.

## Existing tools leave the advanced, self-directed student unserved

The prior art falls into four groups, and each misses for a different reason.

**Course-deployed tutors** all use the same recipe: a system prompt forbidding solutions plus enforcement outside the model. Harvard's CS50 Duck adds a response checker and a rate limit of ten "hearts" that regenerate one every three minutes ([Liu et al., SIGCSE 2024](https://cs.harvard.edu/malan/publications/V1fp0567-liu.pdf)). Toronto's CodeAid restricts each feature's output, for example highlighting incorrect lines with a hover explanation and never showing fixed code ([CodeAid](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.11314v2)). Berkeley's 61A-Bot delivers one-shot feedback inside the autograder and cut homework time by more than 30 minutes per assignment for mid-percentile students, measured against prior semesters and with no learning outcome reported ([61A-Bot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05600)). Prompt-only guardrails leak at double-digit rates: **the Duck produced code blocks in 22% of about 10 million responses and 48% of conversations**, and the rate rose when the underlying model was upgraded ([Liu et al., "Improving AI in CS50"](https://cs.harvard.edu/malan/publications/fp0627-liu.pdf)); CodeAid's "Help Write Code" returned an exact solution in 53% of analyzed cases ([CodeAid](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.11314v2)). All of these tools serve one intro course whose staff wired in the assignment context. None targets a multi-file PyTorch project or a learner without a course staff.

**Vendor learning modes** arrived in 2025 from every major lab. ChatGPT Study Mode is implemented as custom system instructions, which OpenAI says "results in some inconsistent behavior and mistakes across conversations", and it toggles off mid-conversation ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-study-mode/)). Claude Code's Learning output style is the only one inside a real coding workflow, but its premise is inverted for a student: Claude "handles routine implementation itself" and leaves a few lines at a `TODO(human)` marker. Its own documentation says "nothing enforces it" ([Claude Code output styles](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/output-styles.md)). In CS336 nearly everything graded is the implementation, so the student needs to own the code and the AI to own the hints.

**Mainstream editors** solved "AI off" and nothing else. VS Code has `chat.disableAIFeatures` ([VS Code AI settings](https://code.visualstudio.com/docs/copilot/reference/copilot-settings)) and Zed has `disable_ai` ([Zed](https://zed.dev/blog/disable-ai-features)). The research found no shipped student or learning mode in VS Code, Cursor, Zed, Windsurf or JetBrains as of late 2026; this rests on negative search results, not an exhaustive changelog review. Instructors publish manual guides for turning AI off ([Harvey Mudd CS 133](https://www.cs.hmc.edu/~beth/courses/cs133/lab/guides/vscode/index.html)). A student can assemble a rough tutor from an off switch, a read-only chat mode and a rules file, but the rules file is a prompt and leaks, the chat still prints pasteable code, agent mode sits one dropdown away, and nothing persists learner state.

**Teaching editors and startups** offer design lessons more than competition. BlueJ and DrRacket win by removing things and by staging capability ([BlueJ](https://www.cs.kent.ac.uk/pubs/2003/2190/content.pdf); [DrRacket](https://docs.racket-lang.org/drracket/htdp-langs.html)), and Python Tutor ties its AI output back to stepping through code in the visualizer ([UC San Diego Today](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/a-uc-san-diego-tool-teaching-code-to-25-million-is-even-more-critical-in-age-of-ai)). The nearest commercial analogue, CodeTrain, promises never to write "even one line" and gates steps on checks, but it is priced at $24 a month for professionals onboarding to codebases, and its claims come only from its own marketing page ([CodeTrain](https://codetrain.ai/)).

The gap is therefore specific: AI constrained to pedagogy with enforcement outside the prompt, persistent learner state, awareness of a real multi-file ML project and its test runs, adjustable friction, and a deliberately small interface with no agents or orchestration, aimed at an advanced learner working alone.

## Patterns that keep the student thinking beat patterns that slow the student down

The pattern-level research separates cognitive engagement, which helps, from mechanical friction, which only costs time. The table gives the verdicts a builder needs; evidence strength varies and most studies used CS1 novices on short tasks.

| Pattern | What the evidence shows | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Conceptual Q&A and API lookup | Most common high-scoring pattern and nearly as fast as delegation ([Shen & Tamkin](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)) | Include; answer directly |
| Review of code the student wrote | Feedback that locates an issue beats "a test failed" ([arXiv 2407.04873](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.04873v2)); CodeAid's fix help was its weakest feature until it highlighted lines ([CodeAid](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.11314v2)) | Include; line-anchored observations, never a diff |
| Hint ladder | Analogous worked examples were the most productive rung; abstract hints alone misled or annoyed ([Xiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02213v1)) | Include; route by request type, stop before the student's exact code |
| Explain-my-error | Mixed on fix speed; GPT-4 beat the stock compiler message in 1 of 6 tasks ([Santos & Becker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18661v1)) | Include for the message; never the fix |
| Predict and explain prompts | Lead-and-Reveal was the only technique that aligned perceived with actual ability (r = .26) without raising task load ([Kazemitabaar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08922v1)) | Include; skippable, few and hard |
| "Fix this error" loops | Low-scoring and slow; debugging showed the largest quiz deficit ([Shen & Tamkin](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)) | Exclude |
| AI ghost-text autocomplete | Qualitative evidence of drifting and false confidence in novices ([Prather et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02491v1)); CS336 bans it | Off; keep language-server completion |
| Retyping, code puzzles, injected bugs | 1.7x the time with no significant learning benefit ([Kazemitabaar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08922v1)) | Exclude |
| Mandatory Socratic or quiz gates | Least-liked condition in a 2026 RCT ([Eastwood et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29995)); 15 of 42 learners were reluctant to use forced tracing ([Kazemitabaar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08922v1)) | Exclude as mandatory |

Three findings deserve more than a table row. The first is that Socratic questioning helps advanced learners when it targets reasoning and annoys everyone when it targets lookups. In a graduate robotics course, students who used a Socratic tutor showed higher later learning gains while rating it less efficient, though this comes from a preprint read only in summary ([arXiv 2607.03303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03303)). In a 132-student trial, the Socratic condition with full access to the student's code was rated least favorably and showed the highest external-LLM use, the latter not statistically significant ([Eastwood et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29995)). The workable rule is direct, context-aware explanation of concepts and documentation combined with withholding of the assignment's code, with questions reserved for design and debugging.

The second is that an escape hatch is survivable if it is scoped. In an 885-student C lab with an optional "See Solution" button, half never clicked it; students who used it on one or two of three problems scored 72-73 on proctored exams against 73 for non-users, while those who used it on all three scored 66. The result is correlational, and the authors note solution-seeking may reflect a need for help instead of causing poorer performance ([Edugator](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11146v1)). Hard walls fail in practice anyway: 25-35% of participants assigned to a no-AI condition in Anthropic's pilots used AI regardless ([Shen & Tamkin](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20245v1)). For a voluntary learner with every unrestricted tool one alt-tab away, a guardrail is a commitment device the learner chose. The goal shifts from prevention to making the bypass deliberate, visible and recorded.

The third is that comprehension prompts fit ML code unusually well. The main complaint in CS1 studies was trivial questions about simple code. "What is the shape of `x` after this line?" or "what should the loss be at step 0 for a 10,000-token vocabulary?" are non-trivial, checkable by running, and reveal no solution. Questions about a learner's own passing code expose fragile understanding in general: students who struggled to answer questions about their own correct programs earned lower course points ([Lehtinen et al. replication](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.16267v1)). This application to ML code is an inference; no study has tested comprehension prompts on ML or systems code.

## CS336 already wrote the behavioural spec, and its hardest problems are not code generation

CS336 is five implementation-heavy assignments in which the student starts from an empty Python package and connects their own code to provided pytest suites through a glue file, `tests/adapters.py`; initially every test fails with `NotImplementedError` ([assignment1-basics README](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/blob/main/README.md)). Assignment 1 alone has 38 named problems covering a BPE tokenizer, a Transformer, AdamW and a training loop, and it forbids most of `torch.nn`, `torch.nn.functional` and `torch.optim`. Many of its problems are written answers, experiments and resource accounting, submitted as a typeset `writeup.pdf` ([A1 handout](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/blob/main/cs336_assignment1_basics.pdf)). The empty package is deliberate: the student makes every structural decision, which is exactly what code generation erases.

### The policy tightened every year and now ships as a file

In 2024 the course allowed LLMs for low-level programming or high-level conceptual questions but not for solving the problem ([Spring 2024](https://cs336.stanford.edu/spring2024/)). In 2025 it added a strong recommendation to disable AI autocomplete, noting that "AI autocomplete makes it much harder to engage deeply with the content" ([Spring 2025](https://cs336.stanford.edu/spring2025/)). In 2026 it became a rule: **"All your code should be handcrafted by you directly."** AI is permitted "for answering high-level conceptual questions or for providing low-level programming documentation like function signatures and library APIs", and is not permitted "for implementing any part of any assignment", which "includes both coding agents (e.g., Cursor Agents, Codex, Claude Code) and AI autocomplete". Non-AI autocomplete of function names stays allowed. The policy offers one test for any request: "ask yourself whether a CA would comply with your request in office hours" ([CS336 AI Policy](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1SZAlExB1qAc9izHt54gwunNpjKE6wXb8Y7yA_e-baK8/edit?tab=t.0)).

Each 2026 assignment repository carries an identical `AGENTS.md` and `CLAUDE.md` defining the agent's role as "Teaching Assistant, Not Solution Generator". Agents should explain concepts, point to lectures, handouts and documentation, review student-written code in general terms, "help debug by asking guiding questions rather than providing fixes", explain error messages from Python, PyTorch, CUDA and Triton, and "prefer tests and invariants over fixes" such as shape assertions and tiny toy inputs. Agents should not "write any python or pseudocode", "edit code in the student repo", "run bash commands", "point students to third-party implementations", or "give the student the solution or idea for how to solve a problem". When a request crosses the line, the agent should pivot to "explanation, debugging guidance, code review, or a non-pasteable high-level outline" ([CS336 CLAUDE.md](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

This is the strictest and most operational policy among comparable courses. CS224N allows generative AI "as collaborators in the problem-solving process" ([CS224N](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224n/)), and CMU's Deep Learning Systems permits AI coding assistance with no citation ([dlsyscourse.org](https://dlsyscourse.org/)). For an auditor nothing enforces any of it, so the policy works as a self-imposed commitment. One auditor who finished the course wrote his own rules into a `CLAUDE.md` so that asking for a hint would not produce a full solution, and advises deciding early how much LLM help you want ([Andy Timm review](https://andytimm.github.io/posts/cs336/cs336_review.html)). An editor that reads the course's file natively and turns its prohibitions into missing capabilities, not instructions, matches how the course now expects AI to be configured.

### What students actually struggle with

The reported pain is mostly not "I cannot write this function". It falls into four groups, each of which a tool can address without writing a line of solution code.

Silent correctness bugs come first. All tests in `test_tokenizer.py` pass on a tokenizer whose streaming encode splits tokens across chunk boundaries ([issue #88](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/issues/88)). An RMSNorm without a float32 cast overflows in half precision, "a subtle bug that wouldn't be caught by unit tests in float32" ([li-xiang blog](https://blog.li-xiang.xyz/2026/02/09/CS336/CS336-Assignment-1-Building-a-Transformer-Language-Model-from-Scratch)). The handout's own advice is to overfit a single minibatch, inspect intermediate tensor shapes, and monitor activation and gradient norms ([A1 handout](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/blob/main/cs336_assignment1_basics.pdf)).

Second is the question "is this me or the environment?". Snapshot tests fail on Apple Silicon by a maximum absolute difference of 2.3e-05 ([issue #49](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/issues/49)), a GRPO test fails or passes depending on the `transformers` version ([A5 issue #18](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment5-alignment/issues/18)), and the changelog records repeated errata, including three RoPE formula fixes and an AdamW ordering fix ([A1 CHANGELOG](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)).

Third is shape visibility. A self-studier built a VS Code extension this month because "I found myself repeatedly jumping to function definitions to check tensor shapes" and the jaxtyping dimensions were not visible in hovers ([A5 issue #17](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment5-alignment/issues/17)).

Fourth is logistics. Over-allocation on macOS can "cause the entire system to freeze and then reboot" ([A2 issue #11](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment2-systems/issues/11)). Assignment 2's Triton and Nsight work and Assignment 5's vLLM loop need Linux with NVIDIA GPUs, and Assignment 3's training API requires a Stanford ID ([A3 issue #1](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment3-scaling/issues/1)). The 2026 handouts budget compute in B200 hours at about $5-7.50 an hour ([CS336 site](https://cs336.stanford.edu/)). A course TA put self-study spend at around $200 with liberal rental and under $50 with scaled-down problems ([HN thread mirror](https://hn.nuxt.dev/item/48357075)); one auditor spent $353 on RunPod, roughly half on side experiments ([Andy Timm review](https://andytimm.github.io/posts/cs336/cs336_review.html)). That auditor took about 12 weeks at 16 hours a week, and one study group shrank from over 30 learners to 8.

The natural organizing unit is the handout's problem name, which already ties a PDF section to an adapter function, one or more tests and a writeup answer. The course standardizes on uv, pytest with snapshots, jaxtyping, einops, wandb and an IDE debugger, and its native form is scripts and packages, not notebooks ([A1 pyproject.toml](https://github.com/stanford-cs336/assignment1-basics/blob/main/pyproject.toml)).

## A tutor core takes days to build and costs less to run than the GPUs

The build options differ mainly in how much editor one person must maintain. Effort figures below are the research notes' judgement for a part-time solo builder; no comparable-project data was found.

| Approach | Effort to first useful version | What it cannot do | Maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Code plugin (custom output style, deny rules, hooks) plus an MCP tutor server | Days | Custom UI; stopping solution text in chat without an extra check | Low |
| Custom ACP agent hosted in Zed | 1-2 weeks | Inline decorations, progress panels | Low to moderate |
| VS Code extension with its own webview calling the Claude API | 3-8 weeks | Removing or restyling the workbench | Moderate |
| Custom app (Electron or Tauri, CodeMirror 6, language server, terminal) | 2-4 months for an MVP | Debugging, remote and notebooks without large extra projects | High and permanent |
| Fork of VS Code or Zed | Months | Use Microsoft's Marketplace, Pylance or Remote-SSH | Very high |

Three facts decide between them. First, enforcement is available today at the harness level. In Claude Code and the Claude Agent SDK, deny rules apply in every permission mode, `Edit` rules govern all built-in file-writing tools, and adding `Bash` to `disallowedTools` removes the tool from the request entirely ([Agent SDK permissions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/permissions)). That makes "the assistant cannot change files or run commands" deterministic. It does not stop a solution appearing as chat text, which needs a second-pass check on output.

Second, the VS Code AI surface is churning. The Chat Participant API is deprecated and the harness it runs on is scheduled for removal in February 2027; Microsoft's replacement runs inside Copilot's chat, whose default purpose is writing code ([VS Code chat guide](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/extension-guides/ai/chat); [AI extensibility overview](https://code.visualstudio.com/api/extension-guides/ai/ai-extensibility-overview)). An extension that owns its webview panel and calls the model itself avoids both problems and can set `chat.disableAIFeatures` to hide everything else. Zed's extensions cannot add UI, but a custom agent registers in a few lines of settings and Zed supplies the thread interface ([Zed external agents](https://zed.dev/docs/ai/external-agents)).

Third, a fork is the wrong tool at this scale. Forks carry a continuing merge burden ([EclipseSource](https://eclipsesource.com/blogs/2024/12/17/is-it-a-good-idea-to-fork-vs-code/)), cannot use the Microsoft Marketplace ([VSCodium README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/VSCodium/vscodium/master/README.md)), and lose Pylance to licensing ([Cursor forum](https://forum.cursor.com/t/pylance-in-cursor-special-license-or-intentional-inducement-of-copyright-infringement/27794)). A fork's unique benefits are removing UI and making guardrails non-bypassable, and a self-directed student does not need the second.

### Running cost

Current Claude API prices per million tokens are $2 input and $10 output for Sonnet 5.5, $4 and $20 for Opus 5.5, and $0.10 and $0.50 for Haiku 5.5, with cache reads at $0.10 per million on Sonnet 5.5 ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). The research notes estimate a heavy single user at about 1,300 tutor requests a month, each with a 30,000-token cached prefix of course material, 3,000 fresh input tokens and 1,000-1,500 output tokens. That works out to **roughly $30-40 a month on Sonnet 5.5, $65-80 on Opus 5.5, and under $3 on Haiku 5.5**; without caching the Sonnet figure roughly triples. The token counts are guesses and could be off by a factor of two either way. A local model is justified by privacy or offline needs, not cost, and no evidence was found on local-model tutoring quality.

Two constraints follow from Anthropic's terms. Third-party products built on the Agent SDK may not offer claude.ai login and must use API keys ([Agent SDK overview](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/overview)), so anything distributed is bring-your-own-key. A personal build can instead run inside Anthropic's own surfaces on an existing subscription, which is another reason to start with a plugin. All persistent state fits in one local SQLite file, and because current models take a 1M-token context at standard pricing, the current assignment's handout and lecture material can sit in a cached prefix with no retrieval index ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)).

## The product: a quiet editor with a read-only TA

### Design principles

Eight principles follow from the evidence, and each one names the finding it rests on.

The student is the sole author. The assistant has read access to code, tests, run output and course material, and no ability to write or execute. This rests on the delegation findings and on the course's explicit ban on agent edits and commands.

Limits are capabilities, not instructions. Every prompt-only guardrail measured in deployment leaked at double-digit rates, so write-blocking lives in the harness and a second pass checks outgoing text for assignment code.

Help sits one level of abstraction above what the student must produce. For CS336 that means math, shapes, invariants and API pointers, in prose with section references. The course's phrase "non-pasteable" is the acceptance test.

Lookups get direct answers. Syntax, signatures and error-message meanings are answered immediately, because abstract hints and Socratic deflection on lookups irritate and occasionally mislead.

Attempt comes before help. Diagnostic help attaches to a specific failing test or run and asks first for the student's hypothesis. Error resolution stays with the student, since that is where debugging skill formed in the control group.

Passing tests are not understanding. The tool supplies an external check through prediction and explain-back prompts, always skippable.

Friction is calibrated and owned by the learner. The policy level is visible, set per workspace, and read from `AGENTS.md` when present. Overrides are deliberate, scoped and logged.

Subtraction is a feature. The interface has an editor, a test panel, a terminal and one tutor panel, with no agent modes, model pickers or orchestration.

### Feature set

The non-AI workbench carries most of the day-to-day value. It needs language-server completion with AI ghost text absent; a pytest panel that understands the `adapters.py` indirection; a "problem card" for each handout problem linking its PDF section, adapter, tests and writeup answer; jaxtyping-aware hovers that show annotated shapes, plus live shapes from the last test or debug run; snapshot-diff summaries reporting maximum error and fraction mismatched, so a tolerance wobble reads differently from a real bug; one-click profiling with cProfile or py-spy; and a run tracker with wall-clock axes, GPU-hours and a dollar meter. On a laptop, a memory watchdog that kills a runaway process before macOS locks up addresses a repeatedly reported failure.

The tutor panel behaves as office hours. It routes by request type: concept and API questions get direct answers grounded in the checked-out handout version with section and equation references; "why is this test failing" starts with "what did you expect and what happened?" and moves to suggested sanity checks, toy inputs and assertions described in prose; "review my code" returns observations and questions tied to line ranges, never a replacement diff. Its hint ladder runs from orientation through concept to an analogous worked example in a different context, such as a toy shape problem instead of the student's attention function, and has no rung that emits the student's exact code. A triage function checks the course changelog and known issues so it can say that a failure matches a known platform tolerance or erratum.

A thin metacognition layer sits on top. It offers occasional prediction prompts and questions about the student's own passing code, and it keeps a private reliance log built from cheap replicated indicators: help requested before any run, highest hint rung reached per problem, and overrides used per assignment. The log is a mirror for the learner and not an enforcement mechanism, since the causal direction of those indicators is unknown.

The escape hatch exists and is honest about what it is. It becomes available after an attempt, covers one function or failing test, asks for a one-line statement of what was tried, follows the reveal with a comprehension step, and writes to the log. Non-assignment workspaces can run a looser policy.

### What to leave out

The exclusions are as much the product as the features. Leave out AI autocomplete, agent or "apply edit" modes, any "fix it" button on errors, whole-solution generation, and a bottom-out hint that prints the answer. Leave out mechanical friction presented as pedagogy: forced retyping, copy-paste blocking, code puzzles and injected bugs. Because the assistant emits no assignment code, paste barriers have nothing to block. Leave out mandatory Socratic or quiz gates and gamified hint currencies; help-regulation mechanics in tutoring systems changed behaviour without improving domain learning ([Aleven et al.](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-intelli/)). Leave out pointers to third-party implementations, which the course prohibits. Leave out a notebook-first design, a custom debugger and custom remote development in any early version; these are where solo editor projects stall, and existing editors already provide them.

### Minimum first version

The first version is a tutor core with no editor attached, built in days. It consists of the course's `AGENTS.md` extended into a custom tutor prompt with the request routing above; a read-only harness with write tools denied and Bash removed; a cheap second-pass check that blocks responses containing assignment-relevant code; and a small local service holding the SQLite hint log. Host it as a Claude Code plugin for the fastest start, or as a custom agent in Zed if a terminal agent is the thing to avoid, since Zed supplies the chat thread with no UI code and the builder owns the whole harness. Disable edit predictions in the host editor. Use it through Assignment 1 and watch one number: how often you leave for an unrestricted chatbot.

The second version moves the same core behind a VS Code extension with its own panel and a stripped profile, and adds the problem cards, test panel integration and shape hovers. That step gets Python tooling, debugging and remote GPU access for free. A custom minimal editor becomes the right long-term form only if a stripped VS Code still feels like too much, and only if remote work is reduced to an SSH terminal pane.

### Open questions

Several things remain unknown, and the build should treat them as experiments. Whether a guardrailed tutor produces learning gains over no AI, or only parity, is unsettled; the positive results depended on expert-authored content this project will not have. Whether enforcing the concept-only pattern causes the learning seen in self-selected clusters is untested. How well current models comply with the CS336 `AGENTS.md` under pressure is undocumented, so the leak rate of the first version must be measured. The policy's grey zone needs a ruling: naming a bug's location, describing a test without writing it, and generating plotting or sweep boilerplate all sit between the course's "prefer tests and invariants" and its "all experiments you run should be written by you". Generated comprehension questions and review comments are sometimes wrong, and a solo learner has no instructor to vet them. Several VS Code extension API details in the notes, the interaction between Zed's `disable_ai` and its agent panel, and November 2026 GPU prices were not verified. Whether a Spring 2027 offering changes the assignments is unknown.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the product. The scarce thing is not a cleverer tutor prompt; CS336 has already published a good one, and vendors ship several. What is missing is a place where that behaviour is structural: an assistant that lacks the write tool instead of being asked not to use it, in an editor small enough that nothing else competes for attention. Most of the value for a CS336 student then comes from features with no generative AI in them at all, such as visible tensor shapes, test-to-handout mapping, snapshot triage and a cost meter, with the tutor acting as the office hours an auditor does not have.

The honest expectation is that this tool protects learning more than it accelerates it. The evidence says guardrails bring a student back to the no-AI baseline while lowering frustration, and that the remaining risk is defection to an unrestricted chatbot when friction runs too high. That makes the first version's job diagnostic. A week of real use on the BPE tokenizer will show whether the commitment holds, which requests the tutor refuses that it should have answered, and whether the reliance log changes behaviour, and those answers matter more than any choice of editor shell.
